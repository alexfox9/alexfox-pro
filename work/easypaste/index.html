EasyPaste · Case study draft

Making copied text easier to keep and reuse.

A compact macOS utility, shaped around the small decisions that happen between copying text and using it again.

My role

Ideas, research, UX design, AI-assisted development, and testing.

Product

Native macOS app. In final testing; Mac App Store availability is not confirmed.

Narrative draft for local review. Real screenshots and the full research account are still being prepared.

The problem: a copy is easy to lose

When you copy another piece of text, the earlier copy is no longer ready to paste. Some text is useful for a few minutes; some is a reply, link, or phrase you want to keep using.

EasyPaste gives these two needs separate places: History captures copied text, and Note holds text cards you choose to save and edit.

The design had to fit beside other work

The app is a small window used alongside other apps. Limited width, long text, pointer movement, and focus changes all affect the experience. A control can fit visually and still be awkward to reach or unclear to use.

I worked through these details while building and testing the app, with Codex assisting implementation and technical checks. I owned the product ideas and decisions.

Decision 1: make Copy and Paste explicit

Copying changes the clipboard. Pasting also changes another app. Treating them as one action can make it unclear what a click will do.

With Auto paste off, clicking an item copies it. With Auto paste on, a single click selects and a double-click copies and pastes. History’s More → Copy and More → Paste let the user choose a specific action regardless of that setting.

The feedback reports a clipboard write. It does not claim that the destination app accepted the text.

Decision 2: let the preview support the whole task

A truncated row helps scan a compact list, but it cannot show every part of a long item. The preview needs to stay usable while the pointer moves into it, and it should support ordinary scrolling, selection, and copying.

Project testing exposed previews that dismissed too early or interfered with the next click. The interaction was refined so reading a preview could lead into selecting text or acting on the item.

Tags stay in the preview’s header while the text scrolls. Editing a Tag changes its label without changing the copied text.

Decision 3: clarify the two kinds of History

Pinned items have a manual order. Regular History follows recent use. These groups need to be distinguishable without making a narrow list feel crowded.

One iteration used shaded pinned tiles. The expanded list later returned to a neutral background with a small Pinned heading and divider. This kept the grouping while reducing the repeated visual treatment.

Dragging reorders pinned items within their group. Reusing regular text updates its position. The app also works to preserve the visible list position as items move.

What the evidence supports

The project records describe my hands-on testing, feedback, revisions, and automated implementation checks. Those checks helped catch interaction and layout regressions.

This draft does not claim a participant study, measured time savings, improved task-completion rates, or accessibility certification. I’ll add the research methods and material I can share, with clear attribution and limits.

Where the product stands

The current app brings together text History, reusable Note cards, pinning, Tags, Search, full-text previews, and deliberate copy/paste behavior. Final app testing is still in progress.

The next step for this case study is to connect the narrative to real product images and the original research material. The next step for the product is to confirm release, support, and privacy details.
